---
title: Normative economics and value judgements
takeaway: Normative economics asks how the economy ought to be; facts reveal
  consequences but cannot alone decide which values deserve priority.
definition: Normative economics evaluates policies, institutions, or outcomes as
  desirable, better, or required by applying value standards such as equity,
  freedom, efficiency, sustainability, or well-being.
causalChain:
  - Society faces several possible objectives
  - Value judgements assign relative importance to them
  - Positive evidence predicts consequences of alternatives
  - Decision-makers weigh outcomes and reach a normative conclusion
realWorldExample:
  title: Minimum-wage debate combines facts and values
  sourceUrl: https://www.ilo.org/topics-and-sectors/wages/minimum-wages
  observedAt: 2026-09-29
  explanation: International Labour Organization material on minimum wages covers
    wage protection, employment, coverage, and enforcement. Employment effects
    are testable; whether greater wage fairness justifies a cost includes a
    value judgement.
misconceptions:
  - A normative claim need not be illogical or evidence-free; it should disclose
    its value standard and use credible facts.
  - Positive research cannot remove every disagreement because people may attach
    different weights to the same consequences.
examApplication:
  - Identify value language such as should, fair, better, or excessive and state
    the implied criterion.
  - Present positive consequences first, then explain how value weights
    influence the final judgement.
summary:
  - Normative conclusions address what ought to be.
  - Value judgements select and rank objectives.
  - Evidence constrains a judgement without determining its values.
---
Economic policy commonly pursues several goals: efficiency, equity, stability, freedom of choice, and environmental protection. Ranking those goals requires a value judgement and cannot be deduced from data alone.

Normative reasoning can still be disciplined. Positive analysis first estimates the consequences of each option, and the fairness or welfare criterion is then made explicit. This allows disagreement about evidence to be separated from disagreement about values.
