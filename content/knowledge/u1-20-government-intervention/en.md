---
title: Government intervention
takeaway: Government intervention seeks to correct market failure, improve
  equity, or stabilise the economy, but success depends on design, information,
  and implementation.
definition: Government intervention is public-sector action through taxes,
  subsidies, regulation, price controls, public provision, information, or
  macroeconomic policy to alter economic behaviour and outcomes.
causalChain:
  - A market outcome diverges from a policy objective
  - Government selects an instrument suited to the cause
  - The instrument changes prices, rights, or available resources
  - Behavioural responses determine the net social result
realWorldExample:
  title: Tobacco taxation changes the cost of consumption
  sourceUrl: https://www.who.int/initiatives/health-taxes/tobacco
  observedAt: 2026-09-29
  explanation: The World Health Organization presents tobacco tax as a tool to
    reduce tobacco use. It raises the price faced by consumers and can finance
    health priorities, while its effect depends on demand response, enforcement,
    and illicit markets.
misconceptions:
  - The existence of market failure does not prove that any intervention will
    improve the outcome.
  - Intervention costs include administration, compliance, and unintended
    behaviour, not only budget spending.
examApplication:
  - Diagnose the specific market failure or equity objective before selecting a
    policy.
  - Compare policy benefits, risks of government failure, and alternative
    instruments.
summary:
  - Intervention can change prices, rules, and public provision.
  - An instrument should match the source of the problem.
  - Government faces information and incentive constraints too.
---
Markets can omit external effects, underprovide public goods, permit market power, or generate outcomes judged unfair. Government can change the costs and benefits facing private decisions or provide a service directly.

Strong evaluation does not list advantages alone. Public authorities may lack information, face lobbying, or have limited enforcement capacity. Expected gains should be set against administration, avoidance, opportunity cost, and plausible alternative policies.
