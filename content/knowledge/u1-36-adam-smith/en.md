---
title: Adam Smith and laissez-faire
takeaway: Smith explained how specialisation, exchange, and competition can
  coordinate self-interested action, without arguing that law, public goods, or
  moral constraints are unnecessary.
definition: Adam Smith's classical economics treated specialisation, market
  exchange, and competition as important sources of growing wealth.
  Laissez-faire favours avoiding unnecessary state replacement of decentralised
  market decisions.
causalChain:
  - Division of labour increases specialisation and skill
  - Exchange allows specialised outputs to be traded
  - Prices and profit coordinate decentralised choices
  - Competition constrains some private power and encourages adaptation
realWorldExample:
  title: Division of labour remains central to production networks
  sourceUrl: https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/bios/Smith.html
  observedAt: 2026-09-29
  explanation: The Econlib biography of Smith introduces his thinking on
    specialisation, exchange, and institutions. Modern supply chains divide
    tasks across expert firms, gaining productivity while also creating
    coordination and dependency risks.
misconceptions:
  - The invisible hand is not a guarantee that every market outcome is ideal.
  - Smith recognised roles for justice, defence, public works, and education; he
    did not propose abolishing government.
examApplication:
  - Complete the chain from specialisation to productivity, exchange, and a
    wider market.
  - Evaluation of laissez-faire should address competition, public goods,
    externalities, and market power.
summary:
  - Specialisation and exchange support productivity and wealth.
  - Price and competition coordinate decentralised choices.
  - Markets still depend on institutions and public functions.
---
Smith argued that division of labour raises output through dexterity, reduced switching time, and invention of tools. Exchange then lets producers specialise while obtaining other goods through a market.

Laissez-faire is better understood as scepticism toward unnecessary controls and privileges than as the absence of the state. Property, contracts, competition, and public infrastructure enable coordination, while monopoly and concentrated power can undermine it.
