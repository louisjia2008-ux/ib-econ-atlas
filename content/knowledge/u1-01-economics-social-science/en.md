---
title: Economics as a social science
takeaway: Economics studies choice under scarcity and uses models and evidence
  to explain how those choices shape outcomes for other people and society.
definition: Economics is a social science concerned with how households, firms,
  governments, and other agents allocate scarce resources, and with the
  consequences for distribution, efficiency, and well-being.
causalChain:
  - Time and resources are constrained
  - Agents choose within institutions and incentives
  - Individual decisions combine into market or policy outcomes
  - Evidence is used to test economic explanations
realWorldExample:
  title: Comparing economic outcomes across countries
  sourceUrl: https://data.oecd.org/
  observedAt: 2026-09-29
  explanation: The OECD data portal brings together comparable measures of
    employment, income, prices, and policy. Economists can frame an explanation
    about institutions and then examine whether the observed patterns support
    it.
misconceptions:
  - Economics is not confined to money; time, health, education, and
    environmental resources also involve economic choice.
  - A conditional social-science claim is not the same as an unsupported opinion.
examApplication:
  - A definition should connect scarcity, choice, and consequences rather than
    merely say that economics studies the economy.
  - When evaluating a claim, identify how assumptions, evidence, and changing
    human behaviour limit its reach.
summary:
  - Economics examines the allocation of scarce resources.
  - Choice responds to incentives, institutions, and information.
  - Models explain; evidence tests those explanations.
---
Economics starts with decisions made by people and organisations. Households divide time and income, firms choose what to produce, and governments decide how to tax and spend. Each choice takes place where resources are limited and objectives compete.

Societies cannot always be placed in controlled laboratory conditions. Economists therefore combine deductive models with historical comparisons, surveys, natural experiments, and statistical evidence. A careful conclusion is usually conditional—an outcome is more likely under stated circumstances—rather than a law without boundaries.
