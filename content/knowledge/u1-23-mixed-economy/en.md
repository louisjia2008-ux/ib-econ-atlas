---
title: The mixed economy
takeaway: A mixed economy combines market allocation with public rules,
  redistribution, and provision; the central issue is how the boundary is
  designed.
definition: A mixed economy is a system in which private markets and the public
  sector both own resources and share responsibility for production, allocation,
  and distribution.
causalChain:
  - Prices and private agents coordinate many daily choices
  - Law and regulation set market boundaries
  - Taxes, transfers, and public services alter outcomes
  - The institutional mix changes with objectives and evidence
realWorldExample:
  title: Government scale and functions vary across economies
  sourceUrl: https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/government-at-a-glance-2025_0efd0bcd-en.html
  observedAt: 2026-09-29
  explanation: OECD Government at a Glance compares public spending, employment,
    services, and governance. Member economies all use markets while combining
    different degrees of public provision and redistribution.
misconceptions:
  - A mixed economy does not require a fixed fifty-fifty split between market
    and plan.
  - The amount of intervention cannot establish performance without evidence
    about instrument design and outcomes.
examApplication:
  - Use a particular sector to show the distinct roles of market and government.
  - Evaluate an institutional mix against efficiency, equity, stability,
    freedom, and sustainability.
summary:
  - Most actual economies use both markets and government.
  - The mix differs across sectors and over time.
  - Analysis should examine mechanisms and outcomes rather than labels.
---
Most economies allow prices to coordinate large areas of consumption and production while government supplies legal infrastructure, public services, and social protection. The blend in health, education, energy, or transport may differ within one country.

There is no single recipe for a mixed economy. The boundary changes with political values, history, technology, and policy evidence. Useful comparison asks how specific arrangements handle information, incentives, distribution, and accountability.
