---
title: The planned economy
takeaway: A planned economy relies on public authorities to set output, input,
  and distribution targets; it can concentrate resources but faces information,
  incentive, and adjustment problems.
definition: A planned economy is a system in which productive assets are largely
  state-owned or controlled and a central or public planning authority allocates
  resources through administrative targets rather than decentralised market
  prices.
causalChain:
  - The planning authority establishes social priorities
  - Inputs and output targets are assigned to sectors or enterprises
  - An administrative system coordinates production and distribution
  - Feedback informs revisions to later plans
realWorldExample:
  title: State-owned enterprises combine public and commercial goals
  sourceUrl: https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/sub-issues/state-owned-enterprises.html
  observedAt: 2026-09-29
  explanation: OECD work on state-owned-enterprise governance shows how public
    ownership can pursue strategic or service objectives while requiring clear
    mandates, performance information, and accountability to manage conflicting
    goals.
misconceptions:
  - Government ownership of some firms does not make the whole economy centrally
    planned.
  - Planning can concentrate resources, but the centre still needs timely,
    accurate, and local information.
examApplication:
  - Potential strengths include coordinated long-term investment and basic
    services; limitations include information and incentives.
  - Compare degrees and mechanisms rather than presenting market and plan as the
    only two absolute choices.
summary:
  - Public planning replaces much price coordination.
  - Concentrated resources can serve stated social priorities.
  - Weak information and incentives can cause shortage, surplus, and
    inefficiency.
---
Public authorities answer the three basic questions in a planned system. They establish an output mix, production methods, and distribution criteria before communicating resources and targets to producing units.

Central direction can move resources rapidly toward infrastructure or essential services. A complex economy, however, contains large volumes of changing local information. Slow feedback, badly designed targets, or weak incentives to improve can create persistent mismatches.
