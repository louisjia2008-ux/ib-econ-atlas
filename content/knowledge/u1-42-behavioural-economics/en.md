---
title: Behavioural economics
takeaway: Behavioural economics incorporates bounded rationality, self-control,
  and social preferences to explain systematic departures from a fully rational
  benchmark.
definition: Behavioural economics combines psychology and economics to study how
  heuristics, biases, limited attention, self-control problems, and social
  preferences shape economic decisions.
causalChain:
  - Decision-makers face complexity and cognitive limits
  - They use shortcuts and respond to framing
  - Observed choices depart from the standard rational benchmark
  - Behavioural evidence informs richer models and policy design
realWorldExample:
  title: Default options change participation
  sourceUrl: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2017/press-release/
  observedAt: 2026-09-29
  explanation: The 2017 Nobel Prize material summarises Richard Thaler's work on
    limited rationality, social preferences, and self-control. Automatic
    enrolment with an option to leave often changes saving or participation
    rates.
misconceptions:
  - Behavioural economics does not claim that people are always irrational; it
    identifies predictable patterns under particular conditions.
  - A nudge is not automatically benign; choice architecture raises questions of
    transparency, manipulation, and distribution.
examApplication:
  - Identify a specific bias and show how it changes the standard model's
    prediction.
  - Evaluate behavioural policy through effectiveness, freedom, transparency,
    and long-run learning.
summary:
  - Cognitive and self-control limits affect choice.
  - Framing and defaults can change behaviour.
  - Behavioural evidence extends rather than simply replaces standard models.
---
Standard models often represent agents as informed, consistent optimisers with stable preferences. Experiments and field evidence reveal systematic effects from loss aversion, status-quo bias, limited attention, and social norms.

The findings inform both explanation and intervention. A different form, reminder, or default can improve an outcome, but policymakers still need to test responses across groups and preserve informed choice.
