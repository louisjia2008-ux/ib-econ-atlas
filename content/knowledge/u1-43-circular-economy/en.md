---
title: Interdependence and the circular economy
takeaway: A circular economy treats the economy, society, and environment as
  interdependent systems and replaces take-make-dispose with lower material use,
  longer use, and retained value.
definition: A circular economy is a framework for designing and organising
  production and consumption to reduce extraction and waste, retain value in
  products, components, and materials, and support regeneration of natural
  systems.
causalChain:
  - Linear production extracts resources and generates waste
  - Design reduces material use and extends product life
  - Repair, reuse, remanufacture, and recycling create loops
  - Lower primary-resource demand reduces ecological pressure and changes
    business models
realWorldExample:
  title: UN Environment Programme promotes circularity
  sourceUrl: https://www.unep.org/circularity
  observedAt: 2026-09-29
  explanation: UNEP work on circularity links resource extraction to climate,
    biodiversity, and pollution. It emphasises coordinated product design,
    business models, and public policy rather than relying only on end-of-pipe
    recycling.
misconceptions:
  - A circular economy is not simply more recycling; prevention, reduction, and
    reuse usually come first.
  - Circular processes also use energy and resources, so a perfect loop for
    every material is not realistic.
examApplication:
  - Trace resource, value, and waste flows across the whole product life cycle.
  - Evaluate design incentives, infrastructure, consumer behaviour, rebound
    effects, and a just transition.
summary:
  - Economic activity depends on social and natural systems.
  - Circular strategies preserve product and material value before disposal.
  - Firms, consumers, and governments must coordinate the transition.
---
A linear model extracts material from the environment, produces and consumes, and returns residuals as waste. Circular design reduces inputs, makes products maintainable and shareable, supports remanufacture, and recovers materials at the end.

The transition demonstrates interdependence. Firms need collection networks and stable standards, consumers need convenience and information, and government can reshape incentives through producer responsibility, procurement, and pricing. Success depends on life-cycle resource savings rather than a recycling rate alone.
