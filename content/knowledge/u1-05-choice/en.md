---
title: Choice
takeaway: Scarcity makes choice unavoidable; economics asks what guides a
  choice, which alternative is forgone, and who bears the consequences.
definition: Choice is the selection of one option, or a combination of options,
  from two or more feasible alternatives that cannot all be achieved under the
  available resource constraint.
causalChain:
  - Objectives exceed available resources
  - The agent identifies feasible alternatives
  - Expected benefits, costs, and values are compared
  - One course is selected and other uses are forgone
realWorldExample:
  title: Comparing dimensions of a better life
  sourceUrl: https://www.oecdbetterlifeindex.org/
  observedAt: 2026-09-29
  explanation: The OECD Better Life Index lets users change the importance of
    income, housing, environment, health, and work-life balance. It makes clear
    that well-being has competing dimensions and that priorities influence
    choice.
misconceptions:
  - Making a choice does not guarantee the desired result; it means a trade-off
    among feasible options was necessary.
  - An option with no monetary payment may still use scarce time, attention, or
    natural resources.
examApplication:
  - Name the constraint and identify at least two competing uses.
  - Do not stop at saying a choice occurs; state the opportunity cost and the
    affected stakeholders.
summary:
  - Choice follows from scarcity.
  - Comparison involves costs, benefits, and values.
  - Selecting an option rules out at least one alternative use.
---
When objectives cannot all be met, people and societies must establish priorities. Feasible options are limited by income, time, technology, law, and information, so an imagined alternative is not necessarily available in practice.

Economic analysis may compare marginal benefit and marginal cost, but it also asks who chooses, who gains, and who carries the burden. Identical constraints can still produce different decisions because information and value judgements differ.
